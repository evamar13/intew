## Refined Hypothetical Prompt

**You have joined the Communications Impact Unit in the Directorate for Communications. A high-profile OECD report with a statistical release and media push will be published shortly. The Head of Unit asks you to prepare a short internal note explaining how COM should monitor the performance of this output during the first three months after launch. The note should explain how to assess visibility and influence across media, the OECD website, publications, policy and academic uptake, and emerging AI-mediated environments such as generative search and LLM interfaces. It should identify the main measurement and reputational risks, propose a practical set of indicators, explain how bot or crawler activity should be interpreted, and outline how the results should be presented in an internal dashboard or Power BI report for management.**

## Model Answer

### Internal note: monitoring a high-profile OECD release in traditional and AI-mediated environments

The Directorate for Communications should treat this launch as both a communications-performance exercise and a capability-building pilot. The objective is not simply to count attention. It is to determine whether a high-priority OECD output is discoverable, accurately represented, and influential across the channels that matter to the Organisation's mission: media, digital platforms, publications, policy and academic ecosystems, and emerging AI-mediated environments. Because the OECD is a public-policy institution rather than a commercial brand, the framework must privilege credibility, policy usefulness, and methodological caution over novelty or volume alone.

### 1. Objective and monitoring principle

I would recommend a three-month monitoring approach built around one core question: **Is the release reaching priority audiences in ways that preserve attribution, interpretation, and trust?**

This question should be unpacked into four dimensions:

- `Visibility`: was the output surfaced and encountered?
- `Engagement`: did target users go beyond surface exposure?
- `Influence`: did it shape media, policy, academic, or institutional discussion?
- `Risk`: was the content misrepresented, decontextualised, or associated with misleading AI-mediated outputs?

This structure is preferable to a simple traffic-based view because a high-profile OECD release can have limited referral traffic but strong policy uptake, or strong AI visibility but weak attribution and poor interpretive quality.

### 2. Main measurement and reputational risks

The first risk is **false confidence from incomplete attribution**. In AI-mediated environments, users may receive a generated answer influenced by OECD content without ever clicking through to the OECD website. This means low referral traffic cannot be read as low influence.

The second risk is **confusing machine access with audience value**. Bot and crawler activity can show that content is being retrieved, cached, or indexed, but it does not prove that people understood or used the content meaningfully.

The third risk is **misrepresentation**. Generative systems may summarise a release incorrectly, omit caveats, or fail to attribute the OECD properly. For an organisation whose value depends on evidence, neutrality, and trusted statistics, this is not a minor issue. It is a reputational and standards issue.

The fourth risk is **overloading management with noisy indicators**. If exploratory AI metrics are mixed into regular reporting without clear caveats, the OECD risks weakening the credibility of its own communications analysis.

### 3. Recommended monitoring framework

I would use a layered framework that combines validated indicators with a limited set of clearly labelled exploratory AI-era signals.

#### A. Core traditional signals

These are the baseline indicators that should remain central:

- website traffic to the release page and linked assets
- publication downloads
- media coverage volume and prominence
- newsletter or social distribution performance where relevant
- early policy references or institutional uptake
- early academic mentions where visible

These indicators remain important because they are already part of the OECD's communications and impact logic and support comparison over time.

#### B. AI-mediated visibility signals

These indicators should be added as an exploratory layer:

- identifiable AI-related referral traffic where available
- crawler and LLM-related bot activity on priority pages
- page-level access patterns suggesting AI retrieval interest
- periodic qualitative checks of how the release is represented in selected generative search or LLM interfaces
- presence or absence of proper attribution when OECD content is reflected in AI outputs

These signals are useful, but they should be framed as proxies. The purpose is not to claim precise measurement of AI influence, but to detect patterns that are strategically relevant.

#### C. Influence and uptake signals

Because the OECD exists to inform policy and debate, I would explicitly separate attention from uptake:

- policy citations or references in official documents, speeches, and institutional materials
- academic citations or mentions in research outputs where relevant
- references by influential intermediaries such as specialised media, think tanks, or parliamentary stakeholders
- evidence that the release entered policy or expert discussion in member-country contexts

This is critical because a release may have moderate public traffic but still perform strongly against the OECD's real mission.

#### D. Risk and quality signals

I would include a dedicated risk layer:

- inaccurate AI summaries or misattributed claims
- misleading media framings
- significant divergence between official messaging and third-party interpretation
- abnormal traffic patterns that suggest bot distortion
- internal data-quality issues, such as unstable source definitions or duplicate counts

This matters because COM's mandate includes impact and standards, not only distribution.

### 4. How to interpret bot and crawler activity

Bot and crawler activity should be reported, but carefully. I would recommend three distinctions.

First, distinguish **legitimate search and AI crawlers** from suspicious or low-value automated traffic. Not all automation is problematic; some of it is precisely what makes OECD content retrievable in search and AI tools.

Second, distinguish **access** from **engagement**. A crawler visiting a page can indicate discoverability, but not comprehension or influence.

Third, distinguish **retrieval potential** from **actual representation**. A page may be heavily accessed by bots but still be poorly summarised or weakly attributed in AI outputs.

For reporting purposes, I would therefore present bot activity as a contextual signal with two interpretations:

- positive as a sign that important content is being indexed or retrieved
- limited because it is not evidence of user uptake on its own

This is consistent with the broader OECD-style principle that a proxy is useful only when its limitations are made explicit.

### 5. Suggested indicator set

To keep the framework practical, I would propose a small set of indicators.

#### Core KPIs

- visits to the main release page
- downloads of the publication or statistical files
- media mentions in priority outlets
- share of coverage with accurate framing
- early policy references
- early academic references where relevant

#### Exploratory AI indicators

- identifiable referrals from AI-related sources
- volume and share of LLM or crawler traffic on release assets
- number of priority pages frequently accessed by AI-related bots
- results of periodic manual checks in selected AI interfaces
- rate of correct attribution in sampled AI outputs

#### Risk indicators

- number of material misrepresentations identified
- number of attribution failures in sampled AI outputs
- unusual spikes in bot traffic requiring investigation
- indicator-quality issues logged during the reporting cycle

This would provide enough range for management without creating a dashboard that is too dense to use.

### 6. Dashboard and Power BI structure

I would not recommend a standalone AI dashboard at this stage. AI-related indicators should be integrated into the existing communications-performance framework, with a separate page or clearly labelled section for exploratory AI-era measures.

A practical Power BI structure could be:

#### Page 1. Executive summary

- what changed
- why it matters
- key interpretation
- recommended action

#### Page 2. Release performance

- traffic
- downloads
- content-level performance
- distribution by main owned channels

#### Page 3. Media and stakeholder visibility

- media coverage volume
- prominence
- framing
- priority-audience reach

#### Page 4. Policy and academic footprint

- early citations
- institutional references
- signals of uptake beyond media attention

#### Page 5. Emerging AI indicators

- identifiable AI referrals
- bot and crawler patterns
- sampled AI-output checks
- attribution and representation findings

#### Page 6. Methodology and caveats

- source definitions
- known blind spots
- distinction between validated and exploratory indicators
- interpretation guidance

This structure supports two audiences at once: senior managers who need concise conclusions, and analysts who need methodological clarity.

### 7. Governance and cross-team working

The monitoring process should be cross-functional. COM should not try to treat this as a purely isolated analytics task.

The main coordination should include:

- the Communications Impact Unit for framework design, interpretation, and reporting
- digital and web colleagues for site and traffic analysis
- publications colleagues for asset-level performance and metadata quality
- media and newsletter teams for coverage interpretation and distribution context
- relevant policy directorates where early policy uptake or issue sensitivity matters

This is important because AI-era communications intelligence sits at the intersection of measurement, content design, standards, and reputation.

### 8. Data quality and caveats

I would recommend two reporting categories from the start:

- `validated indicators`
- `exploratory indicators`

Validated indicators are those already stable enough for regular management use. Exploratory indicators are still decision-useful, but only if accompanied by clear caveats.

At minimum, the methodology note should document:

- source and definition of each metric
- frequency of collection
- known attribution limits
- how bot traffic is separated from human traffic
- which findings are based on manual checks rather than automated counting

This protects comparability over time and avoids the impression that AI influence can already be measured with precision.

### 9. Immediate recommendation

For the first three months after launch, I would recommend a focused pilot rather than a full system build. The pilot should:

- prioritise one flagship release and its main associated assets
- establish the baseline described above
- test a small number of AI-output checks on selected platforms
- integrate findings into existing reporting rather than creating a parallel workflow
- conclude with a short lessons-learned note on indicator usefulness, data gaps, and next-step improvements

This is a more credible starting point than attempting to build a complete AI-monitoring architecture immediately.

### Conclusion

COM should monitor the release through an integrated framework that combines traditional performance evidence with a cautious AI-era layer. The aim is not to chase every emerging signal, but to answer a strategic OECD question: whether trusted OECD evidence remains visible, correctly attributed, and influential in the environments where users now seek information.

The best approach is therefore a limited but robust pilot: measure core performance, add a small number of proxy indicators for AI-mediated visibility, distinguish machine access from human value, include explicit risk monitoring, and present the results in a management dashboard that is useful, interpretable, and methodologically honest. This would strengthen OECD communications intelligence while remaining aligned with the Organisation's standards of evidence, trust, and public value.
