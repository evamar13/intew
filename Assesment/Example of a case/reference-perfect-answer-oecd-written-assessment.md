## Hypothetical Prompt

**The OECD is seeking to better understand and monitor its visibility and influence in AI-mediated information environments, including generative search and large language model interfaces. Write a short note recommending how the Directorate for Communications should approach this challenge. Your note should identify the main issues, propose a practical monitoring framework, explain how AI-related indicators should be integrated into broader communications reporting, and indicate how dashboards or reporting tools such as Power BI could support this work using inputs from web analytics, media monitoring, and citation data.**

## Model Answer

### Strengthening OECD Communications Measurement In AI-Mediated Environments

The OECD should approach visibility in AI-mediated environments as an extension of its existing communications intelligence function, not as a separate or purely technical exercise. The central issue is not simply whether OECD content appears in generative search or large language model outputs, but whether OECD evidence remains discoverable, accurately represented, and influential in the environments where users increasingly seek information. A strong response therefore requires a framework that is methodologically cautious, strategically useful, and integrated with existing reporting on media, digital, policy, and academic impact.

### 1. Why this matters

AI-mediated environments are changing how users encounter and consume public-policy information. In traditional communications reporting, visibility could be approximated through website traffic, media coverage, downloads, or citation counts. These remain important. However, generative systems introduce new dynamics:

- users may receive an answer without visiting the original source
- OECD work may influence an answer indirectly without producing referral traffic
- machine access patterns, such as bot activity or content retrieval, may not correspond to human engagement
- authoritative content may be surfaced inconsistently across platforms

For the OECD, this matters for three reasons.

First, the Organisation’s mission depends on the reach and uptake of evidence-based analysis. If users increasingly access information through AI systems, the OECD must understand whether its content is present and discoverable there.

Second, visibility alone is not enough. Inaccurate summarisation or weak attribution can create reputational risk, especially for a public-policy institution whose credibility depends on precision and trust.

Third, AI-mediated visibility should inform communications strategy, publication design, and reporting practices. This is therefore not only a technical monitoring question, but a strategic communications issue.

### 2. Main challenges

The OECD should recognise from the outset that measurement in this area will be imperfect. Three challenges are particularly important.

The first is **limited direct attribution**. Some AI-related influence will not appear in referral data, which means that absence of traffic should not be interpreted as absence of impact.

The second is **signal quality**. Bot activity, automated retrieval, and generative mentions can create noisy indicators. Without careful interpretation, this could lead to false conclusions or inflated claims.

The third is **institutional risk**. If exploratory AI indicators are presented as mature performance metrics, this may undermine the credibility of reporting. A distinction should therefore be maintained between validated indicators and experimental ones.

### 3. Recommended approach

I would recommend that the Directorate for Communications adopt a three-layer approach.

#### A. Establish a limited AI monitoring baseline

The first priority should be to define a small, defensible set of indicators that can be monitored consistently over time. These should not aim to capture the full AI ecosystem, which would be unrealistic at this stage, but rather provide enough information to support strategic decisions.

The baseline could include:

- trends in known AI-related referral traffic where identifiable
- LLM and crawler access patterns to priority OECD pages and publications
- page-level analysis of which outputs appear most likely to be retrieved or cited
- periodic manual or semi-structured checks of how high-priority OECD content is represented in selected generative environments
- comparison of AI-era signals with traditional measures such as downloads, citations, and media uptake

The objective of this baseline is not completeness, but comparability and usefulness.

#### B. Integrate AI-era indicators into a broader impact framework

AI-related indicators should not sit in isolation. They should be interpreted alongside existing communications evidence, especially:

- publication performance
- media visibility
- stakeholder uptake
- policy citations
- academic citations

This is important because AI visibility is only one part of communications performance. A publication may generate strong policy uptake with limited generative visibility, or conversely appear often in AI contexts without producing meaningful influence. Reporting should therefore distinguish between:

- visibility
- engagement
- influence
- institutional risk

This distinction would help prevent over-reliance on any single category of signal.

It would also help preserve comparability between data sources that behave differently. For example, Google Analytics or equivalent web-analytics tools may capture visits and referral patterns, while media-monitoring platforms capture press visibility, and citation sources capture longer-term policy or academic influence. These sources should complement each other rather than be read as interchangeable.

#### C. Use monitoring to improve communications practice

The monitoring framework should feed practical improvements in how OECD content is prepared, structured, and reported. In particular, the Organisation could prioritise:

- high-value policy outputs
- pages with strong external demand
- content that is frequently used in media or stakeholder briefings
- publications where accurate machine-readable summaries are especially important

This would support exploratory work on AI discoverability and Generative Engine Optimisation in a way that is consistent with the OECD’s standards of evidence and integrity.

#### D. Build a practical reporting workflow

The Directorate should also ensure that the framework can be operationalised through existing reporting tools rather than remain conceptual. In practice, this means using Power BI or equivalent dashboards to bring together a limited number of trusted inputs from:

- web analytics, for visits, referral patterns, landing pages, and basic audience behaviour
- media monitoring, for coverage volume, prominence, and topic framing
- publication data, for downloads and usage trends
- policy and academic citation tracking, for longer-term influence
- AI-era monitoring, for bot access patterns and selected discoverability checks

The value of the dashboard would not be to display every available metric, but to show decision-makers:

- what changed
- why it matters
- where confidence is high or low
- what action should follow

### 4. Reporting recommendations

I would advise against creating a separate AI dashboard at the outset. Instead, the Directorate should adapt existing reporting frameworks by introducing a clearly labelled section for AI-era indicators.

This section should:

- include only a limited number of indicators
- distinguish between validated and exploratory metrics
- document definitions and caveats clearly
- explain why changes matter in communications terms
- identify where further analysis is required before drawing conclusions

A possible reporting structure could be:

1. **Executive summary**
   What changed, why it matters, and what decision or follow-up is recommended.

2. **Core traditional indicators**
   Downloads, media coverage, policy and academic citations, stakeholder engagement.

3. **Emerging AI indicators**
   AI-related referrals, crawler patterns, qualitative checks of representation, selected discoverability signals.

4. **Interpretation and caveats**
   Confidence level, methodological limitations, and risk implications.

5. **Recommended actions**
   Content, reporting, or communications improvements to test in the next cycle.

This structure would preserve continuity with current reporting while allowing the OECD to build capability in a changing environment.

In Power BI terms, this could be implemented through a small number of pages:

- a senior-management summary page
- a publication performance page
- a media and stakeholder visibility page
- an emerging AI indicators page
- a methodology and caveats page

This would keep the reporting usable while ensuring transparency in how indicators are defined and interpreted.

### 5. Governance and risk management

Because the OECD’s credibility is central to its public value, governance should be built into the framework from the beginning.

In practice, this means:

- documenting methodology for all AI-related indicators
- avoiding public or internal overstatement of uncertain findings
- distinguishing clearly between machine access and demonstrated influence
- distinguishing clearly between bot traffic and human traffic in reporting outputs
- ensuring collaboration between communications, analytics, digital, and policy-facing teams
- reviewing whether AI-related visibility aligns with organisational priorities rather than simply with general online attention

It also means checking data quality systematically across sources. For example:

- ensuring referral classifications are stable
- documenting where attribution is incomplete
- validating whether unusual traffic patterns are automated or human
- applying consistent indicator definitions across reporting cycles

This is also where reputational risk should be considered. The OECD should monitor not only whether its content is visible, but whether it is being represented accurately and in context.

### 6. Conclusion

The OECD should respond to AI-mediated information environments with a measured, integrated approach. The goal should not be to create a parallel measurement system based on immature indicators, but to extend existing communications intelligence so that it remains relevant in a changing landscape.

The most effective approach would be to start with a small baseline of defensible indicators, integrate them into broader reporting on communications impact, and use the findings to improve content discoverability and institutional visibility where it matters most. This would allow the Directorate for Communications to strengthen measurement without sacrificing the standards of evidence, clarity, and trust on which the OECD depends.

In short, the OECD should treat AI-mediated visibility as a strategic communications issue requiring experimentation, but also discipline. A cautious, evidence-based, and action-oriented framework, supported by practical reporting tools such as Power BI and informed by web analytics, media monitoring, and citation evidence, would be the best way to build this capability.
