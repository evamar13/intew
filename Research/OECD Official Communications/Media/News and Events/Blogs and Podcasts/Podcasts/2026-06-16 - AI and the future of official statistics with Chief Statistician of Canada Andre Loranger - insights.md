# AI and the future of official statistics with Chief Statistician of Canada Andre Loranger

## Content Type

- `Podcast`
- Date: `16 June 2026`
- Series: `Statistics in the AI Era`

## Participants

- Host: `Steve MacFeely`
- Guest: `Andre Loranger`

## Core Insights

### 1. AI is changing how people access official statistics

- Users increasingly reach statistics through AI assistants and chatbots rather than directly through statistical office databases.
- This means AI is becoming a new intermediary between society and facts.
- Statistical organisations now need to think about machines as users, not only people.

### 2. Statistical offices need an AI strategy across five fronts

Andre Loranger frames Statistics Canada's approach around five areas:

- using AI to improve operational efficiency
- embedding AI into statistical methods while preserving measurable quality
- measuring the economic and social effects of AI
- making official statistics AI-ready and properly attributable
- rethinking dissemination as AI changes traffic and usage patterns

### 3. Quality in official statistics now needs new dimensions

Traditional quality dimensions still matter:

- relevance
- accuracy
- timeliness
- accessibility
- interpretability
- coherence

But AI adds further requirements:

- traceability
- provenance
- reproducibility

The central implication is that context must travel with the data.

### 4. Metadata is becoming a core strategic asset

- Metadata is no longer just technical support material; it becomes a core dimension of quality.
- Outputs need to be explainable, attributable, and anchored to verifiable sources.
- If AI systems are going to retrieve and restate official statistics, they need strong metadata to interpret them correctly.

### 5. Transparency and human oversight are essential for trust

- Statistics Canada is experimenting with AI in coding, simple text drafting, and other internal uses.
- However, a human remains in the loop for review and final decisions.
- The organisation uses a clear principle: `AI generated, but person verified`.
- Trust depends on being transparent about where and how AI is used.

### 6. The new divide is validated versus non-validated information

- In an AI environment, the key distinction may shift from `official versus non-official` to `validated versus non-validated`.
- This is important because AI can produce plausible but wrong numbers.
- Statistical institutions therefore need to defend validation, rigor, and source quality much more explicitly.

### 7. Communication strategy must evolve with AI

- Before AI, traditional media often acted as the intermediary between official statistics and the public.
- Now AI is taking that intermediary role more often.
- Statistical organisations therefore need different communication strategies for different personas:
  - power users
  - journalists
  - the general public
  - AI systems
- Reaching users where they are becomes more important, including through social media and platform-aware distribution.

### 8. Statistical offices need to monitor how AI uses and cites their data

- Organisations should monitor AI-generated outputs that reference their statistics.
- They should track attribution patterns and misinterpretation risks.
- They should also engage with major AI platforms to improve attribution to official statistics.

### 9. Website and visualisation investment is now an open strategic question

- Bot traffic at Statistics Canada has risen sharply while human traffic has fallen.
- This raises a strategic question: should statistical offices continue investing heavily in websites, data hubs and visualisations if users increasingly consume outputs through AI interfaces?
- Loranger does not present a final answer, but treats this as a major open investment question.

### 10. AI could also help statistical offices understand user demand better

- One opportunity is to learn more about the actual questions users ask AI systems when looking for data.
- If statistical organisations could access that layer of user intent through platforms or intermediaries, they could understand demand better than under the old download-based model.

### 11. The main organisational challenge is cultural, not only technical

- AI literacy should not be limited to a small specialist unit.
- Statistical organisations need a `hub-and-spoke` model:
  - a strong nucleus of data science and AI expertise
  - broad receptor capacity across the rest of the organisation
- Multidisciplinary teams are necessary.
- This is described as a cultural transformation as much as a technical one.

### 12. Future statistical capability depends on a new skills mix

The future workforce needs a blend of:

- subject matter expertise
- statistical and methodological expertise
- AI understanding
- technology and data engineering capability
- metadata design capability
- advanced analytics
- communication skills

Metadata design and data engineering are becoming core statistical competencies.

### 13. Statistics Canada's 2030 vision is data-first, automated, and AI-enabled

- The long-term model is an administrative-data-first vision.
- It emphasises automation, standardised production, modern technology, governance, and multidisciplinary skills.
- AI is being mapped across the full statistical production cycle to identify where it can improve efficiency and delivery.

### 14. AI should be treated as a user, but not the only user

- Loranger does not say AI is now the primary user.
- He does say AI should clearly be treated as one user category.
- This means official statistical products need to be:
  - machine-actionable
  - traceable
  - interpretable by design

At the same time, core statistical values remain unchanged:

- quality
- scientific integrity
- privacy protection
- accountability
